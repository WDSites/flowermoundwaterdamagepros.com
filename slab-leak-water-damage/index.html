How a Slab Leak Happens and Why You Often Miss It

In some slab designs, supply lines run through or beneath the concrete. Over time, a line can corrode, rub against rock or concrete, or fail at a joint. Because the pipe is buried, the leak can run for weeks with no puddle. The water rises through the slab and shows up only where a gap exists, such as under a wall plate or at the edge of the carpet.

A leaking hot water line is the one more likely to give away its location. A warm spot on the floor, or a floor section that feels like it has sun on it with the blinds closed, can point to a hot line under that spot. A cold line leak is quieter and shows up later.

A slab leak is a plumbing failure first and a water damage problem second. We cannot see through concrete, and we do not guess where the pipe is. A plumber with detection gear locates it, and we handle everything the water touched.

Slab Leak Safety: What to Do in the First Hour

  1. Shut off the main water valve at the house or at the meter. This stops the leak completely.
  2. Run one cold tap briefly to relieve pressure, then close it again.
  3. If water is on the floor near outlets, appliances or the panel, stay out. Cut power only from a dry panel while you stand on dry ground.
  4. If you smell gas or see sparking, leave and call 911 or the gas utility before anyone else.
  5. Call a plumber and ask for leak detection by sound, thermal imaging or gas tracing.
  6. Photograph any wet flooring, stains and the meter reading before anything is moved.

Living without water for a day is a nuisance, but leaving the main open is how a slow leak becomes a flooded room. Once the line is isolated, the drying clock starts, and we can begin while the plumber plans the repair.

What We Dry and Remove After a Slab Leak

Water that rises through concrete soaks whatever sits directly on it. Carpet and pad, laminate or vinyl plank and the underlayment beneath are usually the first things wet. Baseboard, the bottom of the drywall and the toe kicks of cabinets wick water upward from the floor.

1

Map the moisture

A thermal camera and moisture meters show how far water spread across the floor and up the walls.

2

Remove wet flooring

We lift carpet, pad and swollen floating floor so the slab can release moisture. Tile that is still bonded is checked first.

3

Open wall bases

Baseboard comes off, and a short strip of wet drywall may be cut away so air can reach the studs.

4

Dry

Air movers, floor drying mats and dehumidifiers run until readings fall. Concrete holds water and releases it slowly, so expect more days than a spill on a wood subfloor.

5

Verify

Readings are logged each visit and compared with an unaffected area before flooring goes back.

Ask a restorer to explain ANSI/IICRC S500 before work starts, since it describes how wet slab-area materials are dried. Most of what we do here is also covered in our structural drying page.

If the leak cannot be repaired right away, we can dry around the active leak after the main is shut, but we cannot finish drying until the plumber has fixed the line. Ask the plumber to coordinate with us.

Plumber Repair Options and How They Affect Drying

A plumber has three broad choices once the leak is found. The first is a spot repair through the slab, where the concrete is opened and the line patched. The second is rerouting the line through the wall or attic so the buried section is abandoned. The third is repiping the affected run.

Each option leaves work for us. Opening the slab adds dust and more wet material, while rerouting sends water-free pipe overhead but still leaves the soaked floor. Tell your plumber about the drying plan before cutting begins, so one side does not undo the other.

Repair work that hides plumbing inside walls or concrete may need inspections. In Argyle, the town requires inspections before plumbing is concealed and again at completion. In Flower Mound, ask the Town of Flower Mound building department what applies to your repair.

Insurance, Gradual Leaks and the Plumbing Split

The Texas Department of Insurance says most homeowner and renter policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, and that gradual leaks and seepage are generally excluded, along with mold they cause. A slab leak can sit between the two, which is why it is a hard claim to predict.

Ask your insurer directly how it treats the plumbing repair versus the resulting water damage. They are often handled as different parts of a claim. Check your policy language on slab and foundation items, and keep your photos, the meter reading and the plumber detection report.

TDI also warns that mold can start growing within a day after a leak, and it advises making temporary repairs and keeping receipts. Shutting the main and drying promptly serve both goals.

Mistakes to Avoid With a Suspected Slab Leak

  • Waiting to see whether the water bill settles down.
  • Gluing new flooring over a floor that still reads damp.
  • Drilling or cutting the slab yourself to chase the leak.
  • Skipping photos of the meter and the plumber report.
  • Leaving the main on at night to keep the taps working.
